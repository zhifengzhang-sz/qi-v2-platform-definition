# The stream: Kafka's guarantees as axioms, and the vendor's stream against them

This file answers one question. The platform publishes each series on a stream, and everything downstream relies on what a stream guarantees. Part 1 takes Apache Kafka's topic partition as the model of a stream and states its guarantees as axioms, each with the exact words of Kafka's documentation and the settings under which it holds. Part 2 lists, one feature at a time, how the stream of bars that the market-data vendor (Massive, formerly Polygon.io) provides for one ticker actually behaves. Part 3 compares the two, axiom by axiom. The difference is the gap that the acquisition component has to close. This file states the gap. It does not design acquisition.

Sources are marked by how each was read: [READ] read in full at the part cited; [SUMMARY] from a search or fetch summary only; [INFER] an inference, not stated by a source. No claim here rests on a [SUMMARY]. Every web source was read as the page's own text, fetched with no login. Kafka's documentation was read at version 4.1, the version the project's other research cites; the site also lists 4.2 to 4.4, which were not compared. Kafka's older wording of the log ("an ordered, immutable sequence") is no longer on Kafka's site, where old versions now show the new introduction; it was read in Kafka's source repository at the tag 2.5.0. Kafka's configuration pages and Javadoc were read at the entries cited, and KIP-32 at the sentences cited, found by a search of its words. Massive's documentation pages were read in their markdown form (each page's address with `.md` added) and its knowledge-base articles as page text; the articles on REST against flat files, on data recency, on Form T trades, on reporting a discrepancy, on VWAP, on ticker changes and on flat-file timing were read from their start to about the first 5,000 to 7,500 characters, and the article on condition codes and the two stock overview pages by a search of their words. The UTP specification (version 4.1, September 2026) was read at its sections 2.3, 3.14, 3.15 and 3.16 and searched elsewhere. The CTA specification (CTS, version 2.11b, January 29, 2026) was read at the Internet Archive's copy, because the CTA's own site refused the request; it was read at its sections 3.6, 6.5 and 6.7 (first paragraphs), its sale-condition table and notes, and its glossary entries, and searched elsewhere. A statement about the definition is marked [READ] where it was checked against `lean/Kafka.lean` or `lean/Contract/Market.lean`.

Words used below. A **bar** is the open, high, low and close price and the volume of the trades of one ticker in one time window (a second, a minute, a day). The vendor calls a bar an "aggregate". The **stream** is the vendor's WebSocket feed, which pushes bars as they are made. The **history** is the vendor's REST endpoint, which returns the bars of a date range on request. A **SIP** is one of the two processors that consolidate the trades of all US stock exchanges into one feed: the CTA's for stocks listed on NYSE and other exchanges, the UTP's for stocks listed on Nasdaq.

## Part 1. The stream's axioms, from Kafka

Kafka stores records in topics, and each topic in one or more partitions. The partition is the unit that has guarantees. Each guarantee below is a candidate axiom of a stream: its statement, Kafka's words, and the conditions under which it holds.

### How the definition models a partition

The definition takes a topic to be "its log: everything ever appended to it". A record is a key, a value, a timestamp and headers, and "its partition and offset are where it is: which log, and which position in it". A record's position in its log stands for its offset: "Kafka's offsets can skip numbers, but their order is the same". Each topic of a working space has one partition and no key. Two things are assumed, not proved: that a reader reads the whole log ("it holds where retention and compaction remove no record before the reader has read it"), and how records travel and are stored (batches, transactions, producer ids, replication). One thing is proved: no process can give a view that replaces a record already given (`Kafka.compaction_not_process`). `lean/Kafka.lean` [READ].

### The axioms

**A1. A partition is one ordered sequence, and it only grows at its end.**
- Kafka's words: "Each partition is an ordered, immutable sequence of records that is continually appended to—a structured commit log." [Apache Kafka 2.5.0, docs/introduction.html](https://github.com/apache/kafka/blob/2.5.0/docs/introduction.html) [READ]. "When a new event is published to a topic, it is actually appended to one of the topic's partitions." [Apache Kafka 4.1, Introduction](https://kafka.apache.org/41/getting-started/introduction/) [READ]. "Our topic is divided into a set of totally ordered partitions". [Apache Kafka 4.1, Design, Consumer Position](https://kafka.apache.org/41/design/design/) [READ]. "The log allows serial appends which always go to the last file." [Apache Kafka 4.1, Implementation, Log](https://kafka.apache.org/41/implementation/log/) [READ].
- Conditions: none on the writer. It is a property of the log as the broker stores it. What can take records away from the front of the sequence is in N3 below.

**A2. A record, once in the log, is never changed in place.**
- Kafka's words: the sequence is "immutable" (the 2.5.0 introduction, above) [READ]. Under compaction, the one background job that rewrites stored files: "Ordering of messages is always maintained. Compaction will never re-order messages, just remove some." and "The offset for a message never changes. It is the permanent identifier for a position in the log." [Apache Kafka 4.1, Design, Log Compaction](https://kafka.apache.org/41/design/design/) [READ].
- Conditions: none. Kafka can remove a record (N3). No page read describes an operation that changes the key or the value of a stored record [INFER, from the absence of one in the pages read].
- The consequence for an update: a new value for something already written can only be a further record. This is what the definition proves as `Kafka.compaction_not_process` [READ].

**A3. Each record sits at a fixed offset. Offsets increase along the log. They are contiguous only under conditions.**
- Kafka's words: "The records in the partitions are each assigned a sequential id number called the offset that uniquely identifies each record within the partition." (the 2.5.0 introduction, above) [READ]. "Kafka maintains a numerical offset for each record in a partition. This offset acts as a unique identifier of a record within that partition, and also denotes the position of the consumer in the partition. For example, a consumer which is at position 5 has consumed records with offsets 0 through 4 and will next receive the record with offset 5." [Apache Kafka 4.1 Javadoc, KafkaConsumer, Offsets and Consumer Position](https://kafka.apache.org/41/javadoc/org/apache/kafka/clients/consumer/KafkaConsumer.html) [READ].
- Where contiguity holds: the part of a log not yet compacted "has dense, sequential offsets and retains all messages." [Apache Kafka 4.1, Design, Log Compaction Basics](https://kafka.apache.org/41/design/design/) [READ].
- Where it does not: "Note that offsets are not guaranteed to be consecutive (such as compacted topic or when records have been produced using transactions)." (KafkaConsumer Javadoc, above) [READ].
- Conditions: fixed and increasing always. Contiguous where the topic is not compacted and no producer uses transactions. The definition does not rely on contiguity: it uses a record's position, which has no holes by construction [READ, `lean/Kafka.lean`].

**A4. Every reader sees the same sequence, in offset order, from whatever offset it starts at, as often as it likes.**
- Kafka's words: "Kafka guarantees that any consumer of a given topic-partition will always read that partition's events in exactly the same order as they were written." and "Events in a topic can be read as often as needed—unlike traditional messaging systems, events are not deleted after consumption." (the 4.1 Introduction, above) [READ]. "All replicas have the exact same log with the same offsets. The consumer controls its position in this log." [Apache Kafka 4.1, Design, Message Delivery Semantics](https://kafka.apache.org/41/design/design/) [READ]. "A consumer can deliberately rewind back to an old offset and re-consume data." (Design, Consumer Position, above) [READ]. "Messages will always be returned in offset order." [Apache Kafka 4.1, Consumer Configs, `isolation.level`](https://kafka.apache.org/41/configuration/consumer-configs/) [READ].
- Conditions: the records are still in the log (N3). A reader sees only records that are safely replicated: "Only committed messages are ever given out to the consumer." [Apache Kafka 4.1, Design, Replication](https://kafka.apache.org/41/design/design/) [READ]. Where a producer uses transactions, the reader's `isolation.level` decides whether it also sees records of aborted transactions; the order is the same either way (Consumer Configs, above) [READ].
- How a reader sets its position, and what it gets from an offset that has been removed, is covered in `docs/designs/research/visualization.md`, section 1.

**A5. The records of one writer are in the log in the order it sent them, also when a send is retried, under the settings named.**
- Kafka's words: "Messages sent by a producer to a particular topic partition will be appended in the order they are sent. That is, if a record M1 is sent by the same producer as a record M2, and M1 is sent first, then M1 will have a lower offset than M2 and appear earlier in the log." (the 2.5.0 introduction, Guarantees, above) [READ].
- The condition under retries: "Note that if this configuration is set to be greater than 1 and enable.idempotence is set to false, there is a risk of message reordering after a failed send due to retries (i.e., if retries are enabled); if retries are disabled or if enable.idempotence is set to true, ordering will be preserved." [Apache Kafka 4.1, Producer Configs, `max.in.flight.requests.per.connection`](https://kafka.apache.org/41/configuration/producer-configs/) [READ]. The same page, under `retries`, says how the order is lost: "if two batches are sent to a single partition, and the first fails and is retried but the second succeeds, then the records in the second batch may appear first." [READ].
- Conditions: the order holds when `enable.idempotence=true` (with `max.in.flight.requests.per.connection` at most 5), or when retries are off, or when `max.in.flight.requests.per.connection=1`. The guarantee is about one producer. The records of two producers writing to one partition are interleaved in the order the broker appends them [INFER, from "sent by the same producer"].

**A6. A retried send does not put the record in the log twice, under the settings named.**
- Kafka's words: the idempotent producer "guarantees that resending will not result in duplicate entries in the log. To achieve this, the broker assigns each producer an ID and deduplicates messages using a sequence number that is sent by the producer along with every message." (Design, Message Delivery Semantics, above) [READ]. `enable.idempotence`: "When set to 'true', the producer will ensure that exactly one copy of each message is written in the stream. If 'false', producer retries due to broker failures, etc., may write duplicates of the retried message in the stream. Note that enabling idempotence requires max.in.flight.requests.per.connection to be less than or equal to 5 (with message ordering preserved for any allowable value), retries to be greater than 0, and acks must be 'all'." and "Idempotence is enabled by default if no conflicting configurations are set." (Producer Configs, above) [READ].
- Its limits: "it is imperative to avoid application level re-sends since these cannot be de-duplicated" and "the producer can only guarantee idempotence for messages sent within a single session." [Apache Kafka 4.1 Javadoc, KafkaProducer](https://kafka.apache.org/41/javadoc/org/apache/kafka/clients/producer/KafkaProducer.html) [READ]. Across sessions it takes a `transactional.id`: "This enables reliability semantics which span multiple producer sessions" (Producer Configs, above) [READ].
- Conditions: `enable.idempotence=true`, `acks=all`, `retries` above 0, `max.in.flight.requests.per.connection` at most 5, one producer session. Kafka removes only the duplicates its own producer makes by retrying. A program that sends the same record twice has written two records.

**A7. A record that has been committed is not lost.**
- Kafka's words: "A message is considered committed only when all replicas in the in-sync replicas (ISR) for that partition have applied it to their log." (Design, Message Delivery Semantics, above) [READ]. "The guarantee that Kafka offers is that a committed message will not be lost, as long as there is at least one in sync replica alive, at all times." (Design, Replication, above) [READ].
- What the writer is told depends on `acks`. `acks=0`: "No guarantee can be made that the server has received the record". `acks=1`: "should the leader fail immediately after acknowledging the record but before the followers have replicated it then the record will be lost." `acks=all`: "This guarantees that the record will not be lost as long as at least one in-sync replica remains alive. This is the strongest available guarantee." (Producer Configs, `acks`, above) [READ].
- Conditions: `acks=all`; a topic's `min.insync.replicas` sets how many replicas must have the record; `unclean.leader.election.enable` off, since turning it on allows a leader "even though doing so may result in data loss" [Apache Kafka 4.1, Topic Configs](https://kafka.apache.org/41/configuration/topic-configs/) [READ]. The definition takes replication and brokers as given [READ, `lean/Kafka.lean`].

### What Kafka does not guarantee

**N1. Record timestamps are not in order.**
- A record's timestamp is, by default, whatever the writer put there. "If the user did not provide a timestamp, the producer will stamp the record with its current time. The timestamp eventually used by Kafka depends on the timestamp type configured for the topic. If the topic is configured to use CreateTime, the timestamp in the producer record will be used by the broker. If the topic is configured to use LogAppendTime, the timestamp in the producer record will be overwritten by the broker with the broker local time when it appends the message to its log." [Apache Kafka 4.1 Javadoc, ProducerRecord](https://kafka.apache.org/41/javadoc/org/apache/kafka/clients/producer/ProducerRecord.html) [READ]. The topic setting `message.timestamp.type` takes `CreateTime` or `LogAppendTime`, and its default is `CreateTime` (Topic Configs, above) [READ].
- The proposal that added timestamps says so directly: "The timestamp might not be monotonically increasing if message.timestamp.type=CreateTime." Under "The good things about LogAppendTime are:" it lists "Monotonically increasing". [KIP-32, Add timestamps to Kafka message](https://cwiki.apache.org/confluence/display/KAFKA/KIP-32+-+Add+timestamps+to+Kafka+message) [READ, at the sentences cited]. The 4.1 pages read state no ordering of timestamps for either type, and the broker's own retention treats them as unordered: it takes "the largest timestamp in a segment file (order of records is not relevant)" (Implementation, Log, above) [READ].
- So the order of a partition is the order of appending, never the order of the times the records carry. A stream whose records must be in order of a date gets that order from its writer, not from Kafka [INFER]. The definition's record has a `timestamp : Int` and no rule on it [READ, `lean/Kafka.lean`]. How the broker bounds a timestamp against its own clock, and what one far-future timestamp does to a reader that tracks the greatest time seen, is covered in `docs/designs/research/acquisition.md`, "A series' order and dates".

**N2. Delivery of a record to a reader exactly once.**
- Kafka names three levels: "At most once –Messages may be lost but are never redelivered", "At least once –Messages are never lost but may be redelivered", "Exactly once –Each message is processed once and only once". "Kafka guarantees at-least-once delivery by default"; exactly once is given when reading from and writing to Kafka's own topics with transactions, and "Exactly-once delivery for other destination systems generally requires cooperation with such systems" (Design, Message Delivery Semantics, above) [READ].
- So the log holds each record once (A6), but a reader may be handed a record again after a failure. What that asks of a reader is covered in `docs/designs/research/warehousing.md`, section 2.

**N3. That a record stays in the log for ever.**
- Retention removes old records: "you define for how long Kafka should retain your events through a per-topic configuration setting, after which old events will be discarded." (the 4.1 Introduction, above) [READ]. `retention.ms` "represents an SLA on how soon consumers must read their data. If set to -1, no time limit is applied." `cleanup.policy`: "The "delete" policy (which is the default) will discard old segments when their retention time or size limit has been reached. The "compact" policy will enable log compaction, which retains the latest value for each key." (Topic Configs, above) [READ].
- Compaction removes a record when a later record has the same key: "The idea is to selectively remove records where we have a more recent update with the same primary key." A reader that starts late then sees less: "Any consumer progressing from the start of the log will see at least the final state of all records in the order they were written." (Design, Log Compaction, above) [READ].
- An administrator can also delete the front of a partition: "Delete records whose offset is smaller than the given offset of the corresponding partition." [Apache Kafka 4.1 Javadoc, Admin, `deleteRecords`](https://kafka.apache.org/41/javadoc/org/apache/kafka/clients/admin/Admin.html) [READ].
- All three remove records and leave the order and the offsets of the rest unchanged (A2, A3). The definition takes the topic to be the whole log and treats what retention or compaction leave as a view of it [READ, `lean/Kafka.lean`].

**N4. Order across partitions.**
- "Kafka only provides a total order over records *within* a partition, not between different partitions in a topic." "However, if you require a total order over records this can be achieved with a topic that has only one partition". (the 2.5.0 introduction, above) [READ]. A topic of a working space has one partition [READ, `lean/Kafka.lean`].

## Part 2. The vendor's stream: its features

The vendor gives the bars of one ticker in three ways. The stream has two channels for stocks: per-minute bars (channel `AM`) and per-second bars (channel `A`). The history is one REST endpoint, `GET /v2/aggs/ticker/{stocksTicker}/range/{multiplier}/{timespan}/{from}/{to}`. The vendor also publishes one file a day (its "flat files"). [Massive, Aggregates (Per Minute)](https://massive.com/docs/websocket/stocks/aggregates-per-minute), [Aggregates (Per Second)](https://massive.com/docs/websocket/stocks/aggregates-per-second), [Custom Bars (OHLC)](https://massive.com/docs/rest/stocks/aggregates/custom-bars), [flat files, Minute Aggregates](https://massive.com/docs/flat-files/stocks/minute-aggregates) [READ].

The bar's fields, against the definition's. The definition's complete bar, `KLine`, has `index` (its place in its series), `date` ("its begin time: milliseconds since 1970-01-01 00:00:00 UTC"), `open`, `high`, `low`, `close` and `volume`. Its bar still going on, `OngoingKLine`, has the same fields and `asOf`, "when its values were last updated" [READ, `lean/Contract/Market.lean`]. A stream message has `s` ("The start timestamp of this aggregate window in Unix Milliseconds"), `e` (the end timestamp), `o`, `h`, `l`, `c`, `v` (the volume, an integer) and `dv` (the volume with fractional shares, a string), and fields about the whole day (`av`, `op`, `a`) and the window (`vw`, `z`). A history bar has `t` ("The Unix millisecond timestamp for the start of the aggregate window"), `o`, `h`, `l`, `c`, `v`, `vw` and `n` (the number of trades). (The three pages above) [READ]. Neither has a place in a series, a version number, a mark that the bar is final, or the time of its last update. The same fields, as a requirement on reading the vendor's form, are in `docs/designs/research/acquisition.md`, "Versions and duplicates of one bar".

The features follow. Each says how the stream behaves, on what evidence, and how long after the bar it can happen. Features 2, 6 and 7 are the three known at the start (bars out of order, bars with errors, corrections that come days later); each is confirmed, with a qualification.

1. **One bar is sent more than once. Each sending is a new version of the same bar.**
   - What the vendor states: "A late or repeated aggregate bar on the WebSocket is deliberate." "Massive holds a late-trade window and rebroadcasts a bar when a late trade changes it. Your consumer should treat a bar with a timestamp it has already seen as a replacement for the earlier one." "When a bar arrives whose timestamp you already hold, overwrite it. Do not append, and do not sum the volume." "A bar you have already stored arriving again is expected behavior, not a duplicate-delivery bug." [Massive, Why am I receiving a late aggregate bar through Massive's WebSockets?](https://massive.com/knowledge-base/article/why-am-i-receiving-a-late-aggregate-bar-through-massives-websockets) [READ].
   - How long after the bar: a per-minute bar stays open to late trades "15 minutes from the minute it covers", a per-second bar "15 minutes from the second it covers" (same article) [READ].
   - Not stated by the vendor: how many versions one bar can have; any mark on the last version; whether a re-sent bar can be equal to the one before it.
   - Also covered in `docs/designs/research/acquisition.md`, "Versions and duplicates of one bar" and "The vendor's own corrections".

2. **Bars do not arrive in order of their dates.** (Known at the start: "bars can arrive out of order". Confirmed for re-sent bars; not stated for first versions.)
   - A re-sent version of an earlier bar arrives after the bars of later windows have been sent. With a 15-minute window, a per-minute bar that arrives can be dated up to 15 minutes before the newest bar already received [INFER, from feature 1 and the 15-minute window].
   - How long after the bar: up to 15 minutes on the stream (feature 1).
   - A late trade in a window that had no bar would make that window's first bar arrive late, as a new bar dated in the past. This follows from "if no eligible trades occur within a given minute, no bar is emitted" and from the late-trade window, but the vendor does not say whether it sends such a bar [INFER].
   - Not stated by the vendor: that the first version of each bar is sent in order of its start time. No page read says so, and no page read says otherwise.
   - What is in order is the transport: "every channel on a connection arrives on one ordered stream", because "A connection is one TCP stream, which delivers bytes in order". [Massive, Why is my Massive WebSocket data running behind, and is it my client or Massive?](https://massive.com/knowledge-base/article/why-is-my-massive-websocket-data-running-behind) [READ]. So a client receives messages in the order the vendor sent them. That is an order of sending, not an order of dates.
   - The history can be asked for in order: its `sort` parameter gives "results in ascending order (oldest at the top)" (Custom Bars, above) [READ].

3. **The first version of a bar may be incomplete, and no message says a bar is final.**
   - What the vendor states, per bar size: the per-second bar is first sent "Two seconds after the second closes, to let late messages arrive"; the per-minute bar is first sent "As the trades arrive and the bar is calculated"; the daily bar is "Continuously recalculated through the session" (the late-bar article, above) [READ]. The post that article points to says of the per-minute stream: "we broadcast the bars as we receive the trades and calculate them. If subsequent trades are received, we recalculate that bar and send it out again." [Massive, Understanding Aggregate Bar Delays](https://massive.com/blog/aggregate-bar-delays) [READ]. A third page says: "Bars are pushed as they close and corrected if a late trade changes them". [Massive, What is the difference between RESTful APIs and WebSockets?](https://massive.com/knowledge-base/article/what-is-the-difference-between-restful-apis-and-websockets) [READ].
   - Not stated by the vendor: whether a per-minute bar is sent before its minute has ended. The three statements above do not settle it. `docs/designs/research/acquisition.md`, "Versions and duplicates of one bar", reads the first of them as a bar sent while it forms; the third reads as a bar sent at its close. This is listed in Part 4.
   - What is certain either way: the message has no field that marks a bar closed or final, and no time of the update (the field lists above) [READ]. A client cannot tell from one message whether a later version will come. It knows only that none comes on the stream after 15 minutes (feature 1).
   - How long after the bar: the first version comes at the close plus 2 seconds for a per-second bar; for a per-minute bar the time of the first version is not stated.
   - Not stated by the vendor: whether the history, asked during the session, returns the bar of the minute now running, and whether that bar is complete.

4. **A window with no eligible trade has no bar. A missing bar is normal.**
   - What the vendor states: "if no eligible trades occur within a given minute, no bar is emitted" (Aggregates (Per Minute), above; the per-second page has the same sentence) [READ]. For the history: "If no eligible trades occur within a given timeframe, no aggregate bar is produced" (Custom Bars, above) [READ]. The stream channels "also send nothing for an empty window". On one heavily traded stock, "885 of its 960 minutes produced a bar. The two sessions before it gave 845 and 792." "Index your series on the timestamp, not on position. Assuming bar n + 1 is one minute after bar n will drift the moment a quiet minute appears." [Massive, How does Massive deal with minutes where no trades occurred?](https://massive.com/knowledge-base/article/how-does-massive-deal-with-minutes-where-no-trades-occurred) [READ]. Per-second bars are sparser: "seconds with no eligible trade produce no bar, so a per-second series is sparse even on a busy ticker." [Massive, Does Massive have per second aggregates available?](https://massive.com/knowledge-base/article/does-massive-have-per-second-aggregates-available) [READ].
   - Three causes look the same on the stream: no trade at all; trades that exist but none eligible to set a price ("Trades that exist but produced no bar means none of them was eligible"); and a closed market (the cause of "A whole day missing" is "A market holiday or an early close"). [Massive, Why are there missing aggregates in Massive's data?](https://massive.com/knowledge-base/article/why-are-there-missing-aggregates-in-massives-data) [READ]. A fourth cause is a lost message (feature 5).
   - How long after the bar: an absent bar stays absent. "Do not treat a gap as an error to retry. Requesting it again returns the same nothing." (the quiet-minutes article, above) [READ]. The one exception is a late trade (feature 2) [INFER].
   - Also covered in `docs/designs/research/acquisition.md`, "Missing bars".

5. **The stream has no sequence number and no replay. A client that was not connected gets nothing of what it missed.**
   - No sequence number on a bar: a bar message has none among its fields (the field lists above) [READ]. The vendor's trade messages do have one, `q`, "increasing and unique per ticker symbol, but will not always be sequential". [Massive, Trades (WebSocket)](https://massive.com/docs/websocket/stocks/trades) [READ].
   - No replay: "a stream only tells you what happened after you connected. The usual pattern is one REST call to load the history or the state you need, then a WebSocket subscription to keep it current, with the stream buffered while the REST call is in flight so nothing falls between the two." (the REST-against-WebSockets article, above) [READ]. After a disconnect, "subscriptions belong to a connection and do not carry over"; a new connection "that authenticates but does not subscribe receives no data". No page read describes a way to ask the stream for a past message.
   - Disconnects are routine: "Connections are reset shortly after midnight Eastern on the stocks, options, indices, forex and crypto clusters." "so a long-running client needs reconnect logic whatever else it does." A slow client is cut off: "the server buffers what you have not read and gives up when that buffer is full". After a drop, "The server takes roughly 10 to 30 seconds to finish releasing a connection it has dropped". By default one connection is allowed per asset class. [Massive, Why do Massive WebSocket disconnects happen?](https://massive.com/knowledge-base/article/why-do-massive-websocket-disconnects-happen), [WebSocket Quickstart](https://massive.com/docs/websocket/quickstart) [READ].
   - What is lost in a gap: the first versions of the bars of that time, and also any re-sent version of the bars of the 15 minutes before it [INFER, from features 1 and 5].
   - A loss does not show on the stream. With no sequence number and with missing bars normal (feature 4), a lost bar looks like a quiet window. Telling them apart takes a request to the history [INFER]. The vendor's own advice is a watchdog: "if a channel that should be busy delivers nothing during market hours, close the connection and reconnect" (the disconnects article, above) [READ].
   - How long: a gap lasts at least as long as the disconnection, 10 to 30 seconds after a drop by the vendor's figure, plus the 15 minutes before it for re-sent versions [INFER].

6. **A bar from the stream can be wrong, by the vendor's own account.** (Known at the start: "bars can have errors". Confirmed for two kinds of error; the vendor states nothing of other kinds.)
   - A trade reported too late is left out: "A trade that arrives after the 15-minute window closes is not folded into the second or minute bar on the stream. It reaches the end-of-day data instead, which is why a minute bar pulled from REST the next morning can differ from the one you received live." (the late-bar article, above) [READ].
   - A trade cancelled or corrected later stays in: "A live bar that included a trade canceled later is not corrected on the stream, which is why it can disagree with the REST bar for the same minute." The stream cannot carry the change: "a cancellation is a separate message that usually arrives well after 15 minutes, and the WebSocket trade message has no field to carry it." (the late-bar article, above) [READ]. Measured delays between a trade and its cancel or correction: 78 minutes, 33 minutes, 39 minutes. How often: "roughly one in 100,000 to 400,000 trades, and every settled session checked had at least one." [Massive, How do Massive's feeds handle canceled trades?](https://massive.com/knowledge-base/article/how-much-does-massives-feeds-handle-canceled-trades) [READ].
   - The vendor's verdict: "REST is the version to trust in both cases" (the late-bar article, above) [READ]. And the history itself is not final for recent days: "A trade pulled from REST for a recent session can therefore gain a correction value later, and a REST bar for that session can still change when it does." (the cancelled-trades article, above) [READ].
   - Not stated by the vendor: any filter for a trade with a wrong price other than the sale-condition rules of feature 10; a rate of wrong bars; any error in a bar that is not a late, cancelled or corrected trade. Rules that check a bar's own values are in `docs/designs/research/acquisition.md`, part 2.2, and were not taken in here.
   - How long after the bar: the stream bar can be wrong from 15 minutes after its window, and stays so. The corrected bar exists only in the history (feature 7).

7. **The vendor supplies corrections in three ways, each with its own delay. After 15 minutes it sends none: the client must ask.** (Known at the start: "the vendor supplies corrections, and a correction can come days after the bar was sent". Confirmed, with this qualification: after 15 minutes a correction is not sent. It appears in the history, and nothing announces it.)
   - Within 15 minutes, on the stream: a re-sent bar, for late trades only (feature 1).
   - After the session, in the history: late trades. A trade later than the window "will not be included in the candle until the end of the day" (the bar-delays post, above) [READ]. "Bars are revised for about 15 minutes on the stream and again at the end of the day." [Massive, How does Massive create aggregate bars?](https://massive.com/knowledge-base/article/how-does-massive-create-aggregate-bars) [READ].
   - Days after the session, in the history: cancelled and corrected trades. "REST bars leave canceled and corrected trades out once the cancellation reaches REST, which happens a few days after the session". The vendor's check: sessions of 4 to 18 September carried the marks, "while the sessions of 21 and 22 September carried none when checked on the 23rd". "a cancellation only shows up there a few days after the session, so a mismatch caused by one appears days later rather than the next morning." (the late-bar and cancelled-trades articles, above) [READ]. The vendor gives no exact number of days.
   - How a client learns of it: by asking again and comparing. "A bar you stored live can be superseded. REST holds the current version, so compare against a fresh pull rather than your stored copy." [Massive, How should I go about reporting a possible Massive data discrepancy?](https://massive.com/knowledge-base/article/how-should-i-go-about-reporting-a-possible-massive-data-discrepancy) [READ]. "A bar that changed after you stored it is a correction, and the REST value is the current one." (the create-bars article, above) [READ]. No page read describes a notice, a list of changed bars, or a date of last change on a bar.
   - The daily files: "the finalized files for a trading day are available for download by approximately 11:00 AM ET the following day", and "a file that is already published can be replaced later, so the copy you downloaded is not always the latest one"; a changed `LastModified` or `ETag` on the file "means the file has been replaced". [Massive, What time are Massive's flat files ready each day?](https://massive.com/knowledge-base/article/what-time-are-massives-flat-files-ready-each-day) [READ]. No limit on how late a file can be replaced is stated.
   - A correction that removes a bar's only eligible trade, or adds a trade to a window that had none, is not described by the vendor. These cases are discussed in `docs/designs/research/acquisition.md`, "The vendor's own corrections".

8. **The history is rewritten when the stock splits. This can happen at any later time.**
   - What the vendor states: history bars are "adjusted for splits" by default; "Set this to false to get results that are NOT adjusted for splits" (Custom Bars, above) [READ]. "an adjusted series is recomputed against today's share count, so the history you downloaded last year no longer matches the history you download today", "and nothing in your data will tell you it moved." "Store the unadjusted prices if you are keeping a permanent record." Nothing is adjusted for dividends. [Massive, Is Massive's stock data adjusted for splits or dividends?](https://massive.com/knowledge-base/article/is-massives-stock-data-adjusted-for-splits-or-dividends) [READ].
   - Both prices and volume change. One minute bar, adjusted for a one-for-three reverse split five days later, "shows three times each price and a third of the volume". With `adjusted=false` the history bar and the daily file's row "agree on every price, the volume and the trade count". [Massive, Why doesn't a bar from the REST API match the same bar in the flat files?](https://massive.com/knowledge-base/article/why-doesnt-a-bar-from-the-rest-api-match-the-flat-files) [READ].
   - How long after the bar: no limit. Every later split changes every earlier bar of the adjusted history.
   - Not stated by the vendor: whether stream bars are adjusted. The stream pages have no `adjusted` field. A stream bar is of trades just made, so it is as traded [INFER].
   - A ticker's name can change too, and the history then sits under two symbols: "the two halves of a renamed company's history sit under two symbols", and "reused symbols are common". [Massive, How does Massive handle ticker changes and acquisitions?](https://massive.com/knowledge-base/article/how-does-massive-handle-ticker-changes-and-acquisitions) [READ].

9. **A bar's timestamp is the start of its window, in UTC, on a fixed grid. The unit differs by feed.**
   - Start, not end: `s` is the window's start and `e` its end on the stream; `t` is the start in the history (the field lists above) [READ]. One history endpoint is the exception: the grouped daily endpoint "defines its t as the end of the aggregate window" (the REST-against-flat-files article, above) [READ].
   - Unit: milliseconds on the stream (`s`, `e`) and in the history (`t`); nanoseconds in the daily files (`window_start`). "getting it wrong is the mistake that actually happens, because it is silent". "A timestamp that decodes to 1970 means the value was divided as if it were seconds. One far in the future means milliseconds were treated as nanoseconds." [Massive, What timezone are Massive's timestamps in?](https://massive.com/knowledge-base/article/what-timezone-are-massives-timestamps-in) [READ]. The vendor's own overview pages disagree with its field tables here: they say "All timestamps are provided as Unix timestamps (seconds since epoch, UTC)". [Massive, Stocks Overview (WebSocket)](https://massive.com/docs/websocket/stocks/overview) [READ]. The field tables and the timezone article say milliseconds; the examples (`"s": 1610144640000`) are milliseconds.
   - Timezone: none in the value. "an epoch integer counts from one fixed instant and carries no zone at all" (the timezone article, above) [READ]. The pages' phrase "in Eastern Time (ET)" is about how windows and sessions are laid out, not about the timestamp.
   - The grid: a minute window begins at "The top of the minute", an hour at the top of the hour, a day at "Midnight Eastern". "Windows are anchored to the start of the day rather than to the session". [Massive, How does Massive treat hourly bars when querying regular trading hours?](https://massive.com/knowledge-base/article/how-does-massive-treat-hourly-bars-when-querying-regular-trading-hours) [READ]. So a daily bar's timestamp is midnight Eastern, a different UTC instant in summer and in winter: "1789012800000 is 2026-09-10 00:00 Eastern, not 09:30, because daily bars are anchored to the start of the day." (the discrepancy article, above) [READ].
   - Sessions: bars "cover pre-market, regular, and after-hours sessions"; pre-market is 4:00 AM to 9:30 AM ET, regular 9:30 AM to 4:00 PM ET, after-hours 4:00 PM to 8:00 PM ET (Aggregates (Per Minute) and Stocks Overview, above) [READ].
   - Not stated by the vendor in a sentence: which of a trade's timestamps puts it in a window. A trade has the time the exchange made it (`participant_timestamp`) and the time the SIP received it (`sip_timestamp`), and "Bucketing on the participant timestamp puts trades in different windows from bucketing on the SIP timestamp" (the create-bars article, above) [READ]. The vendor's worked examples select a bar's trades by `sip_timestamp` (the Form T and VWAP articles, feature 10) [READ]. If that is the rule, it is unclear how a late-reported trade lands in an earlier bar (feature 1) [INFER]. This is listed in Part 4.
   - Against the definition: `KLine.date` is the begin time in milliseconds UTC, which is the stream's `s` and the history's `t` as they are [INFER]. The unit error and the far-future date it produces are covered in `docs/designs/research/acquisition.md`, "A series' order and dates".

10. **Only some trades count toward a bar, and price and volume count different trades.**
    - What the vendor states: "Each bar is constructed solely from qualifying trades that meet specific conditions" (Aggregates (Per Minute), above) [READ]. Each trade carries sale-condition codes, and the codes "decide whether a trade may set the open or the close, extend the high or the low, or add to volume, and one trade can be allowed to do some of those and not the others." "we follow the CTA and UTP guidelines rather than inventing our own rules." (the create-bars article, above) [READ].
    - The rule per code is published as data, in three yes-or-no fields: `updates_open_close`, `updates_high_low`, `updates_volume`. A trade with no condition "updates everything". With several codes, "if every code says false for a field, that trade did not set it". [Massive, How can I tell whether a specific trade updated the OHLCV aggregate bar?](https://massive.com/knowledge-base/article/how-does-massive-create-the-open-high-low-close-volume-aggregate-bars) [READ].
    - An exception the vendor documents: outside regular hours almost every trade carries the Form T code, which the rules list as setting no price, yet "Massive's minute bars before 09:30 and after 16:00 ET take their prices from Form T trades anyway." The daily bar does not: "Form T trades add to its volume and never set its open, high, low or close." [Massive, Does a Form T trade update a pre-market minute bar's high, low, open and close?](https://massive.com/knowledge-base/article/does-a-form-t-trade-update-a-pre-market-minute-bars-high-low-open-and-close) [READ].
    - Consequences. A window can hold trades and have no bar (feature 4). Volume counts more trades than the prices do, so a bar's volume-weighted average price can lie outside its low and high: "A vw outside the high and low of a Massive stock aggregate bar is not a data error." [Massive, How is a stock bar's VWAP calculated, and why can it fall outside the high and low?](https://massive.com/knowledge-base/article/how-is-a-stock-bars-vwap-calculated-and-why-can-it-fall-outside-the-high-and-low) [READ]. Volume can be fractional: the stream's `v` is an integer and `dv` a decimal string (the field lists above) [READ].
    - How long after the bar: not a matter of time. It is fixed when the bar is made, but for cancelled and corrected trades, which the history's bars leave out later (feature 7).
    - "There is no universal source of truth for OHLC data, so Massive and another careful vendor can disagree and both be defensible." [Massive, Why is Massive's market data different from other providers?](https://massive.com/knowledge-base/article/why-is-massives-market-data-different-from-other-providers) [READ].

11. **The stream is real-time or 15 minutes delayed, by plan, on two different addresses.**
    - What the vendor states: the delayed feed is at `wss://delayed.massive.com/stocks` and the real-time feed at `wss://socket.massive.com/stocks`; the plan decides which one a key may use (Aggregates (Per Minute) and WebSocket Quickstart, above) [READ]. The history shows the same split: a delayed key gets `"status": "DELAYED"` on a request that reaches the current day, and its newest bar is 15 minutes older than a real-time key's at the same moment. "An upgrade does not change the address your code connects to". [Massive, I paid for real-time Massive data, so why is it still delayed or refused?](https://massive.com/knowledge-base/article/how-quickly-can-i-access-massives-market-date) [READ].
    - How long after the bar: 15 minutes, always, on the delayed feed.
    - Not stated by the vendor: how the delayed stream treats re-sent bars. The delay and the late-trade window are both 15 minutes. Whether a delayed bar already holds the late trades, and whether re-sent versions are delayed too, is on no page read.

12. **The same bar differs in form between the stream, the history and the daily files.**
    - Fields: the history and the files have the number of trades (`n`, `transactions`); the stream has none. The stream has the window's end and day-wide fields; the history has not (the field lists above) [READ].
    - Precision and unit: history prices are "Rounded to four decimal places", the files have "As many as six decimal places"; the bar time is in milliseconds in the history and in nanoseconds in the files (the REST-against-flat-files article, above) [READ].
    - Values: for one date, the stream bar and the history bar can differ (features 6 and 7), and the adjusted history differs from both (feature 8).
    - So "the same bar from two sources" needs a rule for what equal means [INFER]. The hand-over from history to stream is covered in `docs/designs/research/acquisition.md`, "Versions and duplicates of one bar".

### Why a bar can change: what the consolidated feeds state

The vendor names two causes of a changed bar: a trade reported late, and a trade cancelled or corrected. It does not describe how the exchanges report either. The two consolidated feeds it takes its trades from do.

- A late report is marked on the trade. The CTA feed's glossary: "Sold is used when a trade is printed (reported) out of sequence and at a time different from the actual transaction time." "Sold Last is used when a trade prints in sequence but is reported late or printed in conformance to the One or Two Point Rule." Extended Hours Sold (Out of Sequence) "Identifies a trade that takes place outside of regular market hours and is reported as an extended hours trade out of sequence and at a time different from the actual transaction time." [CTS, Consolidated Tape System Multicast Output Binary Specification, version 2.11b, at the Internet Archive](https://web.archive.org/web/20260908035051/https://www.ctaplan.com/publicdocs/ctaplan/CTS_Pillar_Output_Specification.pdf) [READ]. The UTP feed has the same codes: `L` Sold Last, `Z` Sold (out of sequence), `T` Form T, `U` Extended trading hours (Sold Out of Sequence). [UTP Data Feed Services Specification, version 4.1, section 3.14.1](https://www.utpplan.com/DOC/UtpBinaryOutputSpec.pdf) [READ].
- How late is late: "The U Extended Hours (Sold out of sequence) Sale Condition should be applied when transactions executed during the following time periods are reported more than 10 seconds after execution", the periods being 12:00 a.m. to 9:30 a.m. and 4:00:01 p.m. to 8:00 p.m.; Form T is applied to trades executed in those same periods (UTP specification, section 3.14.5, Notes 1 and 2) [READ]. The vendor's account agrees on the figure: "an off-exchange execution is reported to a FINRA facility rather than matched on an exchange, within 10 seconds during facility hours" (the late-bar article, above) [READ]. The vendor's older post gives another: "FINRA, for example, has up to 15 minutes to report a trade that happened on any of the dark pools" (the bar-delays post, above) [READ].
- A late trade still counts. In both feeds' tables a trade marked Sold (out of sequence) updates the consolidated high and low and the volume, and updates the last price only by exception: when it is "the first or only last sale eligible trade transaction of the business day" (UTP), or "if it is the only qualifying last" (CTA). A trade marked Form T, or Extended Hours Sold (Out of Sequence), updates volume only. With several codes on one trade, "a "no" update for any one of the statistics will take precedence" (UTP specification, section 3.14.2; CTS specification, Sale Condition, with its Notes 2 and 4) [READ]. So a trade that arrives after its bar was sent can change that bar's high, low and volume [INFER].
- A cancel or a correction is a later message that names the earlier trade. UTP: "Any Cancel/Error or Correction messages will include the trade Id of the trade being canceled or corrected in the origTradeId field." (section 2.3) [READ]. CTS: the Trade Cancel/Error message is used "either to report the cancellation of a trade e.g., buyer and seller agrees to cancel a trade, or error a trade that never took place but was reported"; the Trade Correction message names "either the original trade, or, if the original trade has already been corrected, of the most recent correction to the trade" (sections 6.7.9 and 6.7.2) [READ]. So one trade can be corrected more than once.
- It can come on a later day. CTS: "The Prior Day Trade Correction message is used to correct a transaction originally reported on a trading day earlier than the current trading day." "The Prior Day Trade message is used to report a CTA Equity, Local Issue or Bond transaction which occurred earlier than the current trading day and was not reported." The Prior Day Trade Cancel/Error message "is strictly used to cancel/error a trade or correction originally reported earlier than the current day." (sections 6.5.1, 6.5.3, 6.5.5) [READ]. UTP has one message for the same purpose, the Prior Day As-Of Trade, whose action is "As-Of Trade Addition" or "As-Of Trade Cancel" (sections 2.3.5 and 3.16) [READ].
- Not stated by either specification, at the parts read: how many days back a prior-day message may reach.
- Not stated by the vendor: whether it applies prior-day trades and prior-day cancels to the bars of the earlier day, and when. Its account of cancels ("a few days after the session") is about the marks it shows on trades, not about these messages by name.
- Unlike the vendor's stream, the SIP's own feed is numbered and can be replayed for the day: each block carries a sequence number, and "If Data Recipients do not receive a block(s), a retransmission of the missed messages can be requested." (CTS specification, sections 3.5.3 and 3.6) [READ]. The vendor's bar stream passes on neither (feature 5).

## Part 3. The comparison

One row per axiom of Part 1. "Holds" means the vendor's pages state it or it follows from them; "does not hold" means they state the contrary; "not stated" means no page read says either. The last column is an inference in every row: what has to be true between the vendor's stream and a published stream for the axiom to hold of the published one. It says what, not how.

| Axiom | Does the vendor's stream hold it? | Features that break it | Evidence | What has to happen in between [INFER] |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A1. One ordered sequence that only grows at its end | Does not hold. The messages of one connection do arrive in the order sent. But there is no one sequence of bars: each connection starts a new one, and by date the bars go backwards. | 1, 2, 5 | "a stream only tells you what happened after you connected"; a bar is re-sent for 15 minutes; "A connection is one TCP stream, which delivers bytes in order" | The bars read from every connection and from the history are put into one sequence that outlives any connection, and what its order means (the order read, or the order of dates) is fixed by a rule. |
| A2. A record is never changed in place | Does not hold. The vendor's instruction is to overwrite. The history's bar for a date changes after it was first given, and an adjusted history is rewritten whole. | 1, 6, 7, 8 | "overwrite it. Do not append"; "a REST bar for that session can still change"; "the history you downloaded last year no longer matches the history you download today" | Each change of a bar becomes a further record and never an edit; unadjusted prices are what is read, so that a split changes no record already written. |
| A3. Each record at a fixed offset, increasing, contiguous | Does not hold. A bar has no number. Its only identity is its start time, which is shared by all its versions and leaves holes where no trade was eligible. | 1, 4, 5 | no sequence field among a bar's fields; "Index your series on the timestamp, not on position"; "if no eligible trades occur within a given minute, no bar is emitted" | A position is given to each bar when it is written, by the writer's own count; the start time stays a field of the bar and is not its position. |
| A4. Every reader sees the same sequence, from any offset, as often as it likes | Does not hold. The stream cannot be read from a past point. Two clients that connect at different times receive different sequences, and the history asked twice can answer differently. A delayed plan sees the bars 15 minutes later. | 5, 7, 8, 11 | no replay on any page read; "subscriptions belong to a connection and do not carry over"; "compare against a fresh pull rather than your stored copy" | The vendor is read once, by one reader, into a log; everything downstream reads that log and never the vendor. |
| A5. One writer's records are in the order it sent them, also across retries | Not stated for the first version of each bar: no page says they are sent in order of start time. Does not hold for the bars taken together, since a re-sent bar is dated before bars already sent. | 2 | the late-trade window; no statement of sending order on any page read | The published order does not rest on the vendor's sending order; and the writer of the published stream itself keeps A5 by Kafka's settings (`enable.idempotence=true`). |
| A6. No record is in the log twice | Does not hold. A bar with a start time already seen is sent again on purpose. Reading the history after a gap adds bars already received from the stream. | 1, 5, 12 | "A bar you have already stored arriving again is expected behavior, not a duplicate-delivery bug" | A rule decides which version of a start time is the bar of the published series, and where the other versions go; the join of history and stream gives each start time once. |
| A7. A committed record is not lost | Does not hold. Nothing sent while a client is disconnected is sent again, the connection is reset every night, and a slow client is cut off. A loss cannot be seen on the stream, because a missing bar is normal. | 4, 5 | "Connections are reset shortly after midnight Eastern"; "the server buffers what you have not read and gives up when that buffer is full"; "Do not treat a gap as an error to retry" | Every gap in the connection is filled from the history before the series is taken as whole; that the result is the vendor's whole series stays an assumption unless it is checked by reading the history again. |

The vendor's stream holds none of the seven as it stands. The one thing it gives that an axiom can be built on is the order of messages inside one connection.

**What the vendor's stream gives that Kafka's axioms do not speak of.** Each of these is a fact about bars that a stream of records has no word for. Closing the gap means deciding what each becomes in a published stream.

- **Versions of one bar.** Several messages are one bar at different moments. Kafka has records, not versions; nothing in a log says that two records are the same thing (features 1, 7).
- **Which version is final.** The stream's last word comes 15 minutes after the window; the history's comes days later; an adjusted history has no last word at all. No message marks any of them (features 3, 7, 8).
- **A bar still forming.** A bar that will change because its window, or its late-trade window, is still open. The definition has a record for it, `OngoingKLine`, and no topic carries it. Covered in `docs/designs/research/visualization.md`, section 2, and in `docs/designs/research/acquisition.md`, "Versions and duplicates of one bar".
- **Absence as information.** No bar means no eligible trade, or a closed market, or a lost message. A log has no record for "nothing happened", and A3's contiguity is about offsets, not about time (feature 4).
- **The time a bar is about.** A bar has a window on a grid, a session and a calendar. Kafka's only time is the record's timestamp, which it does not order (N1) and which the definition does not use (feature 9).
- **Two beliefs about the same window.** The stream's bar is what was known within 15 minutes; the history's bar is what is known now. They are different facts, and each has a time at which it was learnt (features 6, 7).
- **What a bar is made of.** Which trades count, and that price and volume count different ones, is the vendor's rule, taken from the exchanges' rules. It decides whether a bar exists at all (feature 10).
- **Adjustment.** The same bar in units of today's shares or of the shares of its day. It is a view of the data that moves with every split (feature 8).
- **How fresh the data is.** Real-time or 15 minutes delayed is a property of the subscription, not of any record (feature 11).
- **The ticker over time.** A symbol can be renamed or reused, so the name of the series is not fixed for ever (feature 8).

## Part 4. Gaps

What could not be found or read.

- **Whether a per-minute bar is sent before its minute ends.** The vendor's three statements differ ("As the trades arrive and the bar is calculated"; "we broadcast the bars as we receive the trades and calculate them"; "Bars are pushed as they close"). No page read settles it. It decides whether the first message of a per-minute bar is a forming bar or a closed one. Only watching the stream would settle it, and that takes a subscription.
- **The order in which first versions are sent.** No page read says that bars are sent in order of start time, per ticker or at all.
- **How many versions a bar can have, and whether a re-sent bar can equal the one before.** Not stated.
- **A late trade in a window that had no bar, and a cancel that empties a bar.** Whether the stream then sends a new bar dated in the past, and whether the history then drops the bar, is not stated.
- **Which trade timestamp places a trade in a window.** The vendor's examples use the SIP's time; its late-trade window suggests a trade can be placed earlier than the time it was reported. No sentence states the rule.
- **The delayed stream and the late-trade window.** Whether delayed bars already hold late trades, and whether re-sent versions are delayed, is not stated.
- **The exact delay of the history's corrections.** "End of the day", "the next morning" and "a few days after the session" are the vendor's words. No time of day and no number of days is given, and no page read describes a way to learn which bars changed.
- **The history during the session.** Whether a request that reaches the current minute returns the bar still forming, and whether it holds late trades within the 15-minute window, is not stated.
- **Prior-day trades.** The CTA and UTP specifications define messages that add, correct and cancel trades of an earlier day. How far back they may reach was not found at the parts read, and the vendor does not say whether or when it applies them to bars.
- **Errors other than late and cancelled trades.** The vendor states no filter for wrong prices and no rate of wrong bars.
- **Other clusters.** Everything in Part 2 is for US stocks. "The rebroadcast behavior of the other clusters is not documented", in the vendor's words.
- **The CTA specification** was read at an archived copy, at the parts named in the header; its Automated Retransmission Facility guide was not read. **The UTP specification** was read at the parts named; its sections on sequence numbers and retransmission were not read. FINRA's own trade-reporting rules, the source of the 10-second and 15-minute figures, were not read: the 10 seconds is taken from the UTP specification and the vendor's article, the 15 minutes from the vendor's older post alone.
- **Kafka.** The documentation was read at version 4.1; versions 4.2 to 4.4 were not compared. The phrase "ordered, immutable sequence" is from the 2.5.0 sources and is absent from the 4.1 pages, where immutability rests on the compaction guarantees quoted in A2 and on the absence of any operation that edits a record. The 4.1 implementation page still describes an offset as "giving the byte position of the start of this message", which the Javadoc and the message format contradict (offsets count records); the Javadoc was followed. KIP-32 is a proposal of an early version: its statement that `LogAppendTime` is monotonically increasing was not found confirmed in the 4.1 pages. The transaction protocol, the replication protocol and the protocol guide were not read.
- **Seven of the vendor's articles were read in part** (named in the header). What they say past the part read is not taken in.
